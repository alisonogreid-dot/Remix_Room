Warm-up

Why do you think this period is called “the Enlightenment” — the age of light?

Copied ✓

Thinkers used the light of reason to push back the “darkness” of blindly accepting authority — applying the evidence-based thinking of the Scientific Revolution (Week 46) to society, government and rights. (Notice the metaphor: light vs dark — just like “Dark Ages.” Who gets to call an age bright or dark?)

1Key words (you’ve met these)

Reason — working things out with logic and evidence rather than just accepting what you’re told.

Scientific method — question → hypothesis → test → conclusion.

Empiricism — knowledge comes from observable evidence.

The Enlightenment’s move: apply these to people and power, not just nature.

2The big idea: the social contract

If we use reason, where does a government’s power really come from? Enlightenment thinkers answered with the social contract: an agreement between people and those who rule them.

Security
Hobbes

Without government, life is “nasty, brutish and short.” People hand power to a strong ruler for safety. (Leviathan, 1651)

Rights & consent
Locke

People have natural rights (life, liberty, property). Government rules by consent — and can be replaced if it breaks the deal.

The people are sovereign
Rousseau

“Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains.” Power belongs to the people as a whole. (The Social Contract, 1762)

Separate the powers
Montesquieu

Split government into branches so no one holds all the power — the root of “checks and balances.”

Tolerance
Voltaire

Argued for freedom of speech and religious tolerance; mocked abuses of power and the Church.

Rights for women
Wollstonecraft

Argued women deserve the same reason-based rights and education as men. (A Vindication…, 1792)

3Ideas in motion — the Enlightenment edition

Even the “Age of Reason” ran on things that had travelled. Open each:

Enlightenment ideas were debated in coffee-houses and salons. Coffee came from Ethiopia and Yemen, and the coffee-house itself spread from the Ottoman world into Europe. The “public sphere” had a cross-cultural home.

Edward Jenner’s cowpox vaccine (1796) built on variolation — deliberately exposing people to a mild dose — long practised in China, India and the Ottoman world, and brought to England by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu. The medicine thread from the very first gallery walk pays off here.

Diderot and d’Alembert’s Encyclopédie tried to collect human knowledge in one work — a knowledge system, printed and spread (thanks again to the press).

Comment on “Western ideas.” The Enlightenment is often celebrated as the birth of “Western” values — reason, rights, democracy. Two honest additions for your exhibit: its tools and ideas had cross-cultural roots, and its “universal” rights were unevenly applied — many thinkers left out women, enslaved people and colonised peoples. Wollstonecraft was already pointing this out at the time.

Learning intention

We are learning to formulate a clear, focused research question and an action plan to investigate it — the start of Criterion B.

1What makes a good research question?

A strong question is clear, focused, and about your idea’s journey across cultures. Compare:

Too broad: “What is the printing press?” — a search, not an investigation.
Not about the journey: “Who was Gutenberg?” — a single fact, no cross-cultural story.
Just right: “How did the printing press develop across cultures before it was credited to Europe?” — clear, focused, and about transmission.
